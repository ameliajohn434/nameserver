Module 02

What is a Nameserver & How Does DNS Work?

To fully understand nameservers, one must examine the Domain Name System. Computers communicate across global networks using numerical Internet Protocol (IP) addresses (such as IPv4 192.0.2.1 or IPv6 2001:db8::1). Because memorizing numbers for hundreds of websites is impractical, inventors created text-based domain names.

A nameserver is a specialized server that stores DNS records (such as A, CNAME, MX, and TXT records) and responds to queries from client browsers and recursive resolvers. When a visitor enters a web address, a query travels through hierarchical tiers:

  1. Recursive Resolver: Your Internet Service Provider (ISP) or public resolvers (like Google 8.8.8.8 or Cloudflare 1.1.1.1) receive your request.
  2. Root Name Servers: The resolver queries root servers which direct traffic based on the Top-Level Domain (TLD) such as .com, .org, or .net.
  3. TLD Name Servers: TLD servers direct the query to the specific authoritative nameservers assigned to your domain.
  4. Authoritative Nameservers: Your host's nameservers return the exact IP address of your web server, loading the site in milliseconds.

Primary Nameserver

Stores the master zone file for your domain. All zone record edits, additions, and deletions are initially authored here.

Secondary Nameserver

Maintains exact replicas of zone files via secure zone transfers, ensuring high availability and zero downtime if primary hardware fails.

Module 03

The Role of a Domain Registrar

A domain registrar is an ICANN-accredited commercial entity authorized to manage domain name reservations within global registries. Popular examples include GoDaddy, Namecheap, NameSilo, and Google Domains (Squarespace).

It is vital to distinguish between a domain registrar and a web hosting provider. Your registrar holds the lease to your domain name, whereas your web host stores your website files and database. You do not need to host your website with the same company where you bought your domain. By modifying your nameservers at your registrar, you tell the global registry to route traffic to your chosen hosting provider's DNS cluster.

Step-by-Step Guide: How to Change Nameservers

While interfaces differ across hosting and registrar platforms, the operational workflow follows a strict universal sequence. For visual walkthroughs and platform-specific screenshots, consult how to change nameservers.

1

Log into Your Registrar Dashboard

Access the client portal of the company where you purchased your domain. Enter your secure credentials, complete multi-factor authentication (MFA), and locate your active domain portfolio.

2

Access Domain Settings & DNS Controls

Click on the specific domain name you want to modify. Navigate to tabs labeled Manage Domain, DNS Settings, or Nameservers. Ensure any active "Registrar Lock" is temporarily disabled if restricted.

3

Input Custom Nameserver Hostnames

Switch the nameserver option from "Use default registrar nameservers" to "Custom Nameservers". Carefully enter the hostnames provided by your web host:

Nameserver 1: ns1.yourhostingprovider.com
Nameserver 2: ns2.yourhostingprovider.com
4

Save Changes & Verify

Click Save Changes or Update Nameservers. Re-enable your domain security lock if necessary, and prepare for global DNS propagation.

Module 06

Understanding DNS Propagation Timelines

One of the most frequent questions web administrators ask is: "Why is my website not loading immediately after updating nameservers?" The answer is DNS propagation.

When recursive DNS resolvers query your domain, they cache (store) previous records to accelerate web browsing speeds globally. When you update your nameservers, these distributed caches do not clear simultaneously. Instead, they expire incrementally based on the Time To Live (TTL) values previously assigned.

Global propagation typically takes anywhere from 2 to 24 hours. During this transition, some regional visitors will access your new server while others still view your legacy site. You can monitor live global resolution status using online lookup tools.

Module 07

Security & Best Practices

Because DNS controls global routing for your digital assets, maintaining strict security protocols is vital:

  • Enable Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA): Protect your domain registrar and web hosting accounts with app-based TOTP authentication.
  • Activate Registrar Locks: Prevent unauthorized domain transfers or malicious nameserver hijacking by keeping registrar locks enabled when not actively making changes.
  • Pre-Configure Zone Files: Set up your A, CNAME, and MX records on your new hosting provider before switching nameservers to eliminate downtime and email disruption.
Module 08

Troubleshooting Common Errors

If your domain fails to resolve after 24 hours, review these common diagnostic items:

Typographical Errors

Check for trailing blank spaces or misspelled hostnames (e.g., ns1.hosting.com vs ns1.hosting.co).

Active Registrar Lock

Some registrars silently reject custom nameserver updates if the domain privacy or transfer lock is engaged.

Local DNS Cache Stale

Flush your local operating system DNS cache using ipconfig /flushdns (Windows) or sudo dscacheutil -flushcache (macOS).

Frequently Asked Questions

Will changing nameservers disrupt my business email?

Yes, if MX records are not duplicated on your new hosting DNS zone before switching nameservers. Always recreate your mail records beforehand.

Do I need to transfer my domain to change nameservers?

Never. You can keep your domain registered with any registrar while pointing your nameservers to any web hosting provider worldwide.
